# Evidence and clinical-content rationale

Reviewed: **23 October 2026**. This document records a targeted review of clinical guidelines, published reviews, and professional patient instructions. It is not an exhaustive systematic review, a clinical validation of the website, or an individualized treatment prescription.

## Intended use and limits

Vestibular Trainer is an educational timing and visual-target aid for adults with **diagnosed peripheral vestibular hypofunction**, including unilateral loss following vestibular neuritis, whose clinician has recommended gaze-stabilization practice. It is not intended to diagnose dizziness, replace a vestibular assessment, or provide a complete rehabilitation program. Balance, gait, habituation, and other treatment components may be needed.

The principal guideline concerns objectively diagnosed peripheral hypofunction. Its recommendations cannot simply be generalized to unexplained dizziness, central vestibular disorders, or every condition causing vertigo. The interface therefore asks users to follow their clinician’s exercise selection and progression rather than diagnosing a stage of disease or prescribing a course.

## Main guideline and dosage

Hall CD, Herdman SJ, Whitney SL, et al. **Vestibular Rehabilitation for Peripheral Vestibular Hypofunction: An Updated Clinical Practice Guideline From the Academy of Neurologic Physical Therapy of the American Physical Therapy Association.** *Journal of Neurologic Physical Therapy*. 2022;46(2):118–177. DOI: [10.1097/NPT.0000000000000382](https://doi.org/10.1097/NPT.0000000000000382). [PubMed PMID 34864777](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/34864777/) · [Full text, PMC8920012](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8920012/).

The guideline strongly supports vestibular rehabilitation and supervision for peripheral hypofunction. Action Statement 6b gives **weak recommendations** for gaze-stabilization dosage, combined with weekly clinic visits:

| Condition | Home gaze-stabilization dose |
| --- | --- |
| Acute/subacute unilateral hypofunction | At least 12 minutes total daily, in 3 sessions |
| Chronic unilateral hypofunction | At least 20 minutes total daily, in 3–5 sessions, for 4–6 weeks |

These are daily practice totals, not set lengths. The guideline does not establish a universally optimal X1:X2 allocation, horizontal:vertical allocation, set count, or rest interval. It also strongly recommends against isolated smooth-pursuit or saccadic eye exercises as gaze-stability treatment for this population. Head movement is essential to the exercises implemented here.

## Why the app uses sample settings

The initial settings are **30 seconds per set, two sets per direction, and 30 seconds of rest**. A standalone X1 or X2 sequence has four sets and 2 minutes of active time. Combined has eight sets and 4 minutes of active time. Instruction and rest time are excluded from these active totals.

This arrangement is an adjustable product example, not a regimen established by the guideline or a trial. The interface does not call Combined a universally recommended session or infer that everyone should repeat it a certain number of times daily. A user’s clinician may recommend shorter or longer bouts, a different frequency, only X1, or an entirely different program.

Rest can be extended in 30-second increments. At the end of the countdown the user must choose to continue; the target does not automatically restart. This implements symptom-dependent recovery without implying that 30 seconds is a medically sufficient rest for every user. Brief instructions appear for five seconds before the first set and each change of movement. Direction cues remain visible during exercise, and the research panel holds the full setup and safety guidance.

## Mechanics, target, and viewing distance

- **X1:** a stationary target with smooth, small head turns horizontally or vertically. Eyes remain on the target.
- **X2:** the target and head move in opposite directions while the eyes remain on the target. It is a progression used when recommended by the clinician, not a mandatory component for every person with neuritis.
- A high-contrast **E** provides recognizable detail so the user can notice blurring. The cue is to slow down if the target is unclear, using a comfortable head range.
- The setup suggests a stable chair, an eye-level screen, and approximately **1 metre / 3 feet** viewing distance, unless the clinician specifies otherwise. This is a practical near-target setup, not a proven optimum for every person or display.
- Target travel and speed are expressed by browser animation on a screen. Pixels and animation timing do not measure physical visual angle or head velocity. Screen size, zoom, distance, and actual head movement vary. The website does not track head/eye motion or verify correct performance.

The [ANPT patient-exercises resource](https://neuropt.org/practice-resources/anpt-clinical-practice-guidelines/peripheral-vestibular-hypofunction-clinical-practice-guideline/patient-exercises) links an official **VOR X1 Instruction Card**. The card was retrieved and its text verified. It uses a target a few feet away, smooth small head movements and a clear target. Duration, repetition count, and daily frequency are blank for individual prescribing. It advises contacting the therapist for a symptom increase lasting more than 20 minutes, or following the therapist’s instructions.

[Sherwood Forest Hospitals NHS, *Vestibular Rehabilitation Management Plan*, printed pages 6–7](https://www.sfh-tr.nhs.uk/media/2kxnsa40/pil202512-04-vrmp-vestibular-rehabilitation-management-plan.pdf) describes fixed-target horizontal and vertical practice and progression to opposite head/target movement. It includes one-minute practice examples and recovery between exercises. This supports the mechanics and recovery approach, not the app’s particular 30-second preset. Earlier pages contain different exercises; those instructions are not applied to this gaze-stabilization trainer.

Han BI, Song HS, Kim JS. **Vestibular rehabilitation therapy: review of indications, mechanisms, and key exercises.** *Journal of Clinical Neurology*. 2011;7(4):184–196. DOI: [10.3988/jcn.2011.7.4.184](https://doi.org/10.3988/jcn.2011.7.4.184). [PubMed PMID 22259614](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/22259614/) · [Full text](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3259492/). The review corroborates near/far targets, horizontal/vertical head movements, and opposite-direction moving targets. The newer guideline takes priority for the dose discussion.

## Symptom and safety language

A mild, temporary increase in dizziness can occur. The app instructs users to stop for pain, severe dizziness, nausea, or feeling unsafe, and to let symptoms settle before continuing. The ANPT 20-minute advice is a reason to contact a clinician, **not an instruction to tolerate severe symptoms for 20 minutes**. Unusual or concerning symptoms warrant earlier advice.

The brief emergency guidance is based on [NHS, *Vertigo*](https://www.nhs.uk/conditions/vertigo/): new weakness, speech difficulty, double vision or vision loss accompanying sudden vertigo require emergency assessment; severe headache, severe vomiting, and sudden hearing loss warrant urgent medical care. Exercise is not an assessment for new symptoms.

## Additional and newer literature checked

- **Huang HH et al., 2024.** *Efficacy of Vestibular Rehabilitation in Vestibular Neuritis: A Systematic Review and Meta-analysis.* *American Journal of Physical Medicine & Rehabilitation*. 103(1):38–46. DOI: [10.1097/PHM.0000000000002301](https://doi.org/10.1097/PHM.0000000000002301). [PubMed PMID 37339059](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37339059/). The abstract reports 12 randomized trials involving 536 patients and supports rehabilitation for neuritis. It does not validate this app’s set allocation or animation.
- **Agger-Nielsen HE et al., 2024.** *Early vestibular rehabilitation training of peripheral acute vestibular syndrome—a systematic review and meta-analysis.* *Frontiers in Neurology*. 15:1396891. DOI: [10.3389/fneur.2024.1396891](https://doi.org/10.3389/fneur.2024.1396891). [PubMed PMID 38872828](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38872828/). The abstract supports early rehabilitation in the studied clinical setting; it is not a basis for self-diagnosis or a universal combined session.
- **Fushiki H et al., 2026.** *Vestibular rehabilitation strategies of the Japan Society for Equilibrium Research.* *Auris Nasus Larynx*. 53(3):332–340. DOI: [10.1016/j.anl.2026.03.005](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.anl.2026.03.005). [PubMed PMID 41850198](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41850198/). Abstract reviewed: supports multimodal rehabilitation for chronic unilateral hypofunction. Full text was not reviewed, so no exact dose is inferred from it.
- **Surano S et al., 2026.** *Internet-based vestibular rehabilitation versus written instructions after acute vertigo: A randomised controlled trial.* *PLOS ONE*. 21(6):e0351092. DOI: [10.1371/journal.pone.0351092](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0351092). [PubMed PMID 42284306](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/42284306/). Abstract reviewed: the internet program was not superior to written instructions on the primary outcome. This reinforces avoiding claims that a digital format itself improves clinical outcomes.

## Review method and remaining uncertainty

PubMed searches covered the updated peripheral-hypofunction guideline, vestibular-neuritis rehabilitation reviews, newer guideline/review literature, and gaze-stabilization mechanisms. The guideline’s abstract and relevant full-text sections, the Han review full text, official ANPT instructions, and NHS practical/safety references were inspected. Newer articles listed above were checked at abstract level unless stated otherwise. This search did not screen all literature, assess every study’s bias, or establish that no other relevant publication exists.

There has been no clinical evaluation of this implementation, no measured physical calibration of its moving target, and no professional review of its fitness for a particular patient. Sources are cited for the clinical principles they support; citation does not imply endorsement of the site. Avoid adding cure claims, an automatic progression schedule, inferred adherence, or a universal treatment dose without appropriate new evidence and clinical review.
